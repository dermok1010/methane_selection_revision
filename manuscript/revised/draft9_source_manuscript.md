## Abstract

### Background

Enteric methane, produced during rumen fermentation and released mainly by eructation, is a major challenge for environmentally sustainable ruminant production. Genetic selection can deliver long-term and cumulative reductions in emissions; however, methane production is positively correlated with key performance traits such as feed intake, body size and growth. Ratio- and residual-based methane definitions have therefore been proposed. The objectives of this study were to estimate genetic parameters for a range of alternative methane trait definitions in sheep, to characterise the genetic and phenotypic relationships between them and with key production traits, and to examine their use in a selection index.

### Results

In total, 16,535 methane (CH₄) records from 8,354 sheep across 132 Irish flocks were analysed. Alternative methane traits were defined as ratios of methane relative to metabolic body weight (MBW), average daily gain (ADG), muscle mass and rumen volume, and as residuals of methane regressed on these traits. Heritability estimates ranged from 0.12 to 0.46. Daily methane was positively genetically correlated with MBW (rg = 0.72), live weight (0.71) and ADG (0.51). Ratio and residual definitions were negatively genetically correlated with their denominator or adjustment traits, while the residual traits remained strongly genetically correlated with absolute methane (rg = 0.78–1.00). In a selection index of CH₄, MBW and carbon dioxide (CO₂), a favourable response, reducing methane by 0.61 g day⁻¹ with no decline in MBW or CO₂, was reached from every methane trait once appropriate weights on MBW and CO₂ were included in the breeding goal, so the weighting rather than the trait definition determined the outcome.

### Conclusions

Methane in sheep shows meaningful additive genetic variation across trait definitions. Ratio and residual methane traits remained strongly genetically correlated with absolute methane but differed in their genetic relationships with body size and growth, and therefore in the correlated responses expected in production traits. Methane can be reduced within a selection index without undesirable responses in other important traits, but the outcome is determined by how methane and these traits are weighted in the breeding goal rather than by the choice of methane trait definition.

## Background

Reducing greenhouse gas emissions from ruminant livestock has become a priority for greenhouse gas mitigation strategies worldwide. Enteric methane emissions (hereafter referred to as methane emissions), produced during rumen fermentation, contribute approximately 30% of global anthropogenic methane emissions \[1\], making it one of the largest and most challenging emission sources to address \[2\]. At the same time, rising global demand for animal-sourced protein also places increasing pressure on ruminant production systems to deliver sustained reductions in emissions without compromising productivity \[3\]. The trade-off between greenhouse gas mitigation and food production is particularly evident in grass-based systems, such as Ireland, where agriculture is the largest single sector source of national greenhouse gas emissions, with methane from enteric fermentation in cattle and sheep the dominant gas \[4\]. Concurrently, despite this high sectoral methane output, Ireland ranks among the most carbon-efficient regions globally for ruminant production when evaluated on a per-kilogram-of-product basis \[5, 6\]. This places particular emphasis on mitigation strategies capable of delivering sustained reductions in enteric methane emissions without reducing productivity or altering the fundamental structure of pasture-based systems.

Genetic selection for low-emitting animals has emerged as one of the most promising of these methane mitigation strategies, with moderate heritability reported for methane production \[7\] and the cumulative, long term nature of genetic gain \[8, 9\]. The recent development of high-throughput phenotyping technologies, most notably portable accumulation chambers (PACs), has enabled routine, large-scale collection of individual-animal methane phenotypes across both research and commercial flocks \[10, 11\]. PACs allow groups of animals to be measured in about an hour under field conditions \[12, 13\]. Methane measurements from PACs are heritable (0.13–0.20 in grazing sheep) \[10–12\] and genetically correlated with respiration chamber (RC) methane (rg = 0.62–1.00) \[10, 14\]. PACs also record carbon dioxide (CO₂), a proxy for feed intake where intake cannot be measured directly \[15\]. Although each record is a short spot measurement with lower repeatability than RCs \[10, 13\], PACs rank animals accurately \[16\] and consistently across grazing and controlled environments \[11\], which is what genetic selection requires. Methane production has also been shown to have strong (positive) genetic and phenotypic correlations with key production traits such as body weight, growth, and feed intake \[7, 17–19\]. As a result, direct selection for reduced absolute methane emissions risks inadvertently constraining animal performance.

The development of alternative methane trait definitions allows emissions to be quantified in a way that is less directly tied to an animal's body size and growth. Traits such as methane intensity, methane yield, and residual methane express emissions relative to an animal's metabolic demands or level of performance \[18, 20\]. These traits can identify animals that emit less methane than expected for their level of productivity and have therefore been proposed as a potential means of mitigating unfavourable genetic correlations between methane production and key production traits \[21, 22\]. Phenotypic analyses have shown that these alternative methane metrics can differ substantially in their relationships with production traits and with each other \[23\]. Both ratio and residual methane traits present known theoretical limitations, and, as noted by Lassen and Difford \[24\], the genetic parameters of these alternative definitions remain underexplored. The present study therefore estimates genetic parameters for alternative methane traits in sheep, including their relationships with each other and with key production traits, and explores how they respond to selection.

## Methods

### Gaseous emissions data

A total of 16,535 methane measurements were obtained from 8,354 sheep across 132 research and commercial flocks in Ireland between 2019 and 2025. Methane measurements were recorded using PACs following the protocol described by O'Connor et al. \[25\]. Animals were removed from feed at least one hour prior to measurement. Each animal was then randomly assigned to one of twelve PAC units. Once the chamber was sealed, gas concentrations were measured at entry and again after a 50-minute accumulation period using an RKI Eagle 2 gas monitor (Weatherall Equipment and Instruments Ltd., UK). Concentration changes for methane (CH₄) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) were converted to daily output (g day⁻¹) using chamber-specific calibration equations.

Methane (g day⁻¹) and carbon dioxide production (g day⁻¹) measurements were first screened for outliers using a 1.5 × interquartile range criterion applied separately to both gases (n = 511 (3.09%) gaseous records removed). All gaseous records were assigned to a contemporary group of date of measurement, flock, and PAC measurement group (i.e., the same 12 animals measured at the same time-point). Only contemporary groups with at least 5 animals were retained, leaving 15,869 records from 8,185 animals.

Measurements were collected across multiple physiological stages, including lambs (both sexes, 63 to 365 days of age; 5,564 records from 3,034 animals), hoggets (nulliparous females aged 365 to 600 days; 1,812 records from 1,271 animals), and mature ewes (females with a recorded lambing event aged 431 days to 10.5 years; 7,828 records from 4,327 animals). A total of 25.40% of animals had multiple methane records both within or across physiological stages.

### Additional phenotypic data

Additional phenotypic measurements were available for animals with methane records; these included live weight, average daily gain (ADG), muscle mass, and rumen volume, and were used to derive alternative methane traits.

#### Live weight

Live weight was recorded immediately prior to PAC measurement using a Prattley electronic weighing scale (O'Donovan Engineering Co. Ltd., Cork, Ireland) and was subjected to outlier removal (1.5 × interquartile range criterion). Metabolic body weight (live weight⁰·⁷⁵) was also derived for each animal. A total of 15,638 records from 8,002 animals were available for analysis.

#### Average daily gain

Average daily gain was derived for growing animals only (i.e. animals <660 days of age). All individual animal live weight records were obtained from the national sheep database operated by Sheep Ireland. For each methane measurement, live weights recorded within ±120 days of the PAC measurement date were considered. Average daily gain surrounding each methane measurement was estimated by fitting a linear regression of live weight on age in days, with the regression slope retained as the estimate of ADG (kg day⁻¹). A total of 4,316 ADG estimates were available from 2,658 growing animals after outlier removal (1.5 × interquartile range criterion).

#### Rumen volume and muscle mass

Individual animal computed tomography (CT) scan measurements taken within ±3 days of methane measurement were also available on a subset of animals. Computed tomography provides in vivo estimates of carcass tissue composition by classifying pixels in cross-sectional X-ray images according to their density, allowing fat, muscle and bone tissues to be quantified non-invasively as described by Clelland et al. \[26\]. From these scans, estimates of rumen volume (litres) and muscle mass (kg) were available. Following outlier screening (1.5 × interquartile range criterion), 766 CT measurements were available for both rumen volume (746 animals) and muscle mass (751 animals).

#### Additional data

Auxiliary data relating to animal age, breed composition, animal heterosis and recombination loss, and birth and rearing type were also available. The breed proportion of each animal for the six breeds with the greatest number of methane phenotypes (i.e., Belclare, Charolais, Cheviot, Suffolk, Lleyn, and Texel) was also calculated, together with the combined proportion of all other or unknown breeds. The general heterosis and recombination loss coefficients for each animal were also included \[27, 28\]:

```latex
het = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} sire_i \times dam_i
```

```latex
rec = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{sire_i^2 + dam_i^2}{2}
```

where sireᵢ and damᵢ are the proportion of breed i in the sire and dam, respectively. For all growing animals the birth litter size (defined as whether the lamb was born as a single, twin, triplet or quadruplet), rearing litter size (defined as whether the lamb was reared as a single, twin or triplet), and dam parity (defined as 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 or ≥7) were available. For ewes the birth and rearing type corresponding to the litter born to the ewe for the year of measurement were also available.

### Methane trait definitions

A suite of methane and CO₂ traits was constructed (Table 1), comprising absolute gas traits (daily CH₄ and CO₂ production) and two groups of alternative methane traits: ratio-based traits and residual traits. In addition to daily (absolute) methane production (CH₄, g day⁻¹), a series of alternative methane traits were investigated, including ratio traits, where CH₄ was divided by a production or physiological trait, and residual traits, where CH₄ was regressed on one or more of these traits.

#### Absolute Gas Production

Methane production (CH₄) measured in g day⁻¹ represented the total mass of enteric methane, released via eructation during rumen fermentation, emitted per animal per day as captured by PAC measurement. Carbon dioxide production (CO₂), measured concurrently on the same PAC record and also expressed in g day⁻¹, was also analysed.

#### Ratio-Based Methane Traits

For all animals, two ratio traits were calculated: methane per kg of metabolic body weight, representing methane intensity (CH₄/MBW; g day⁻¹ kg⁻⁰·⁷⁵), and the other representing methane as a proportion of total methane and carbon dioxide production (CH₄ ratio). CH₄ ratio was calculated on a mass basis (g/g) as CH₄/(CH₄+CO₂), with both gases expressed in g day⁻¹. For growing animals only, an additional three ratio traits were also computed: methane per kg of average daily gain (CH₄/ADG; g kg⁻¹), methane per kg muscle mass (CH₄/MM; g day⁻¹ kg⁻¹), and methane per litre rumen volume (CH₄/rumen; g day⁻¹ L⁻¹).

#### Residual Methane Traits

Three residual methane traits (RMT) were derived: methane adjusted for metabolic body weight (RMTMBW), methane adjusted for metabolic body weight and carbon dioxide production (RMTMBW+CO₂), and methane adjusted for average daily gain (RMTADG; calculated in growing animals only). Residual methane traits were generated by regressing daily methane production (g day⁻¹) on the production trait of interest (metabolic body weight, carbon dioxide production; and where available, average daily gain). The residuals from each regression were extracted and used as the phenotype for the corresponding residual methane trait.

### Genetic analysis

Variance components for all alternative methane traits were estimated using pedigree-based animal models fitted in ASReml version 4.2 \[29\]. Pedigree records were available for 36,449 animals (all phenotyped animals plus their recursively traced ancestors) and were used to define the additive genetic relationships among animals; 100% of phenotyped animals were represented in the pedigree, 88.5% had both parents known, the pedigree comprised 1,025 unique sires with a mean pedigree depth of 17.6 generations, and 73.1% of sires linked in at least two contemporary groups. All methane traits were analysed using the following linear mixed animal model:

*y = μ + sex + BR + CL + CV + LY + SU + TX + UN + het + rec + age + BTg + RTg + BTₑ + RTₑ + DP + CG + a + pe + e*

where *y* was the phenotypic observation for the methane trait under analysis; *μ* was the overall mean; *sex* was the fixed class effect of sex (male or female); the fixed effects for breed proportion corresponding to Belclare (*BR*), Charolais (*CL*), Cheviot (*CV*), Lleyn (*LY*), Suffolk (*SU*), Texel (*TX*), and the combined proportion of all other or unknown breeds (*UN*), were included as covariates; *het* was the heterosis coefficient; *rec* was the recombination loss coefficient; *age* was the age at measurement (weeks of age); *BTg* was the fixed class effect of birth litter size of the growing animal itself (single, twin, triplet or quadruplet); *RTg* was the fixed class effect of rearing litter size of the growing animal itself (single, twin or triplet); *BTe* was the fixed class effect of birth litter size recorded for the ewe in the year of measurement (single, twin, triplet or quadruplet); *RTe* was the fixed class effect of rearing litter size recorded for the ewe in the year of measurement (single, twin or triplet); *DP* was the fixed class effect of dam parity for growing animals; *CG* was the contemporary group fixed class effect of flock-date-lot of measurement; *a* was the random animal additive genetic effect; *pe* was the non-additive random animal permanent environmental effect; and *e* was the residual random error term, where ***A*** was the numerator relationship matrix, ***I*** was the identity matrix, *σ*²ₐ was the direct additive genetic variance, *σ*²ₚₑ was the animal permanent environmental variance, and *σ*²ₑ was the residual variance.

This model described above was used for both univariate and bivariate analyses. As animals of different ages and sizes were recorded, residual variance was expected to differ between contemporary groups, increasing with the group mean \[30\]. To account for heterogeneous residual variance, contemporary groups within each flock were ranked by mean CH₄ and split into three classes (low, medium and high methane emitters), and a separate residual variance was fitted for each flock × class combination (41 classes; flocks with fewer than three contemporary groups contributed fewer classes). This heterogeneous residual model was used for CO₂ and for the five methane traits recorded across all physiological stages (CH₄, CH₄/MBW, CH₄ ratio, RMTMBW, RMTMBW+CO₂), and its estimates are reported for these traits, with heritability (h²) and repeatability (t) calculated for each class and averaged across classes weighted by the number of records. For the four traits restricted to a single physiological stage or the sparse computed-tomography subset (CH₄/ADG, CH₄/MM, CH₄/rumen, RMTADG), a single homogeneous residual variance was used. For CH₄/MM, CH₄/rumen and RMTADG, the permanent environmental effect was omitted from the model because of the small number of repeated records (the permanent environmental variance converged to zero). Bivariate analyses assumed a homogeneous residual variance.

Heritability (h²) and repeatability (t) were calculated as:

```latex
h^2 = \frac{\sigma^2_a}{\sigma^2_a + \sigma^2_{pe} + \sigma^2_e}
```

```latex
t = \frac{\sigma^2_a + \sigma^2_{pe}}{\sigma^2_a + \sigma^2_{pe} + \sigma^2_e}
```

where σ²ₐ, σ²ₚₑ and σ²ₑ are as defined above; where the permanent environmental effect was omitted, t is not reported. Genetic and phenotypic correlations were obtained from the corresponding fitted (co)variance components, with standard errors derived by the delta method; computations were implemented in R version 4.5.3 \[31\].

### Selection index analysis

To investigate the impact of methane traits on the response to selection, a selection index including CH₄, MBW and CO₂ was constructed. The selection index analysis examined how absolute, ratio and residual methane traits themselves operate within an index. The aim was to understand the behaviour of these traits, not to estimate the response to selection under a national breeding index.

Genetic parameters were taken from a trivariate animal model of CH₄, MBW and CO₂ fitted in ASReml \[29\] to the same 15,869 records, with the fixed effects of the univariate model, and with animal permanent environmental effects assumed uncorrelated between traits. Phenotypic variances (σ²ₚ) are the sums of the additive, permanent environmental and residual components, and are therefore conditional on the fixed effects.

Index weights were derived using Smith–Hazel selection index theory \[32, 33\], with CH₄, MBW and CO₂ recorded on each selection candidate. The index weights are **b** = **P⁻¹Ga**, where **P** and **G** are the phenotypic and additive genetic (co)variance matrices of the three traits and **a** contains the weight on each trait in the breeding goal. These goal weights are relative weights, not economic values: they define each methane trait as a combination of CH₄, MBW and CO₂, or are chosen to hold the MBW and CO₂ responses at zero, and only their ratios affect the index and responses. The predicted response in each trait is Δ*G* = *i*·***Gb***/√(***b***′***Pb***), where *i* is the selection intensity. Responses are reported per generation for a selection intensity (i) of 1. Responses for i = 1.7 (approximately the top 10% of candidates selected) and annual responses (assuming a generation interval of 2.8 years) are given in Supplementary Table S2.

Each methane trait was expressed as a breeding goal with weights on CH₄, MBW and CO₂. Absolute methane placed a weight on CH₄ only. The residual traits deducted MBW and CO₂ from CH₄ using either the genetic regression coefficients (genetic residual; 0.833 per kg⁰·⁷⁵ and −6.09×10⁻⁵ per g day⁻¹) or the phenotypic regression coefficients (phenotypic residual, corresponding to RMTMBW+CO₂; 0.423 per kg⁰·⁷⁵ and 0.00371 per g day⁻¹). The ratio traits CH₄/MBW and CH₄ ratio (CH₄/(CH₄+CO₂)) were linearised around the trait means using a first-order Taylor series approximation \[34, 35\], which is equivalent to deducting 0.810 × MBW and 0.0151 × CO₂ from CH₄, respectively. Linearised at the means, CH₄ ratio and CH₄/CO₂ give the same goal direction (0.0151 = mean CH₄ / mean CO₂), so the results for CH₄ ratio apply equally to CH₄/CO₂.

The responses attainable by any index of CH₄, MBW and CO₂ were first mapped (Figure 1), and a favourable region was defined as the set of responses in which CH₄ declined and neither MBW nor CO₂ declined. For each methane trait, the additional goal weights on MBW and CO₂ needed to reach a reference favourable response were then derived; the reference response was defined as the largest methane reduction attainable with MBW and CO₂ both held at zero response (Kempthorne and Nordskog \[36\]; related to the desired-gains approach \[37\]). For each methane trait, these weights and the resulting responses are given in Table 6.

Uncertainty in the index results was quantified by Monte Carlo simulation. The 15 (co)variance parameters of the trivariate model were resampled jointly from a multivariate normal distribution, using their REML estimates and the sampling (co)variance matrix from ASReml, so that both the standard errors of the estimates and the correlations among them were accounted for. For each of 10,000 samples, **G** and **P** were rebuilt and the index results recomputed. Results are reported as point estimates with 95% intervals (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the samples).

The analysis assumes that genetic parameters are constant across generations, that selection is on the candidate's own records (no relatives' records or genomic information), and that all three traits are recorded on every candidate. Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.3 \[31\].

## Results

Fixed-effect significance (Wald F-tests) and diagnostics of pedigree completeness and connectedness are presented in Supplementary Material S1. Texel and Suffolk proportions varied substantially within contemporary groups, whereas Cheviot and Lleyn proportions were largely confined to particular contemporary groups, so their breed effects cannot be cleanly separated from contemporary group effects. Most contemporary groups (82.1%) contained a single sex. The mean MBW was 22.10 (SD = 4.92) kg, mean live weight was 62.72 (SD = 18.18) kg, while mean ADG, muscle mass, and rumen volume were 0.18 (SD = 0.10) kg day⁻¹, 11.7 (SD = 3.24) kg, and 6.16 (SD = 1.49) L, respectively. Descriptive statistics for methane traits are presented in Table 1. Mean methane production was 17.9 (SD = 7.56) g day⁻¹, with methane intensity (CH₄/MBW) averaging 0.81 (SD = 0.29) g day⁻¹ kg⁻⁰·⁷⁵. Mean carbon dioxide production was 1,189.6 (SD = 534.2) g day⁻¹ (range 40.9–2,869.1). Methane expressed relative to average daily gain (CH₄/ADG) had a mean of 137.9 (SD = 189.3) g kg⁻¹, reflecting the small denominator in this ratio trait. The mean values for methane traits derived from CT measurements were 1.44 (SD = 0.48) g day⁻¹ kg⁻¹ for CH₄/MM and 2.69 (SD = 0.88) g day⁻¹ L⁻¹ for CH₄/rumen. Residual methane traits were centred around zero by construction, with phenotypic standard deviations of 6.53 g day⁻¹ for RMTMBW, 5.32 g day⁻¹ for RMTMBW+CO₂, and 4.75 g day⁻¹ for RMTADG.

### Genetic parameters

Genetic parameters for the methane and CO₂ trait definitions are presented in Table 2. Genetic standard deviations ranged from 0.06 g day⁻¹ kg⁻⁰·⁷⁵ for methane intensity (CH₄/MBW) to 35.92 g kg⁻¹ for methane expressed relative to growth rate (CH₄/ADG), with corresponding genetic coefficient of variation (CVa) values of 7.44% and 26.05%, respectively. Methane production (CH₄) had a genetic standard deviation of 1.47 g day⁻¹ and CVa of 8.20%, and carbon dioxide production (CO₂) had a genetic standard deviation of 117.18 g day⁻¹ and CVa of 9.85%. The genetic standard deviation for methane expressed relative to muscle mass and rumen volume were 0.20 g day⁻¹ kg⁻¹ and 0.37 g day⁻¹ L⁻¹ with CVa values of 13.59% and 13.83%, respectively. Residual methane traits (RMTMBW, RMTMBW+CO₂, and RMTADG) had genetic standard deviations of 1.29, 1.41, and 1.55 g day⁻¹.

Heritability estimates ranged from 0.12 ± 0.03 for CH₄/ADG to 0.46 ± 0.11 for CH₄/MM (Table 2). Heritability estimates for CH₄ (0.19), CH₄/MBW (0.16), CH₄ ratio (0.19), RMTMBW (0.16) and RMTMBW+CO₂ (0.17), from the heterogeneous-residual model, and for CH₄/ADG (0.12 ± 0.03) and RMTADG (0.28 ± 0.03) were broadly similar. Carbon dioxide production (CO₂), also from the heterogeneous-residual model, was somewhat more heritable than most methane traits (0.25; repeatability 0.43). Methane expressed relative to muscle mass and rumen volume produced larger heritability estimates (0.46 ± 0.11 and 0.35 ± 0.11), although both were associated with larger standard errors, consistent with their much smaller sample sizes (766 CT records). Where the permanent environmental variance could be estimated, repeatability ranged from 0.20 (CH₄/MBW) to 0.43 (CO₂).

### Correlations among methane traits

Genetic and phenotypic correlations between daily methane production (CH₄), CO₂, and each alternative methane trait definition are presented in Table 3. Phenotypically, daily methane was strongly correlated with the size-adjusted definitions (0.87 with both CH₄/MBW and CH₄/MM, 0.90 with RMTMBW, 0.79 with RMTMBW+CO₂ and 0.71 with CH₄/rumen) and essentially perfectly correlated with RMTADG (1.00), but more weakly with CH₄/ADG (0.31) and CH₄ ratio (0.46).

#### The favourable region

Figure 1 shows every response attainable by an index of CH₄, MBW and CO₂. The favourable region within it, in which CH₄ is reduced without a decline in MBW or CO₂, exists, and along its boundary the correlated CO₂ response was positive (+10.1 to +64.5 g day⁻¹). The region is small, covering about 6.5% (95% interval 4.9–7.8%) of the area of attainable responses in Figure 1, because CH₄ is strongly genetically correlated with both MBW and CO₂. Holding both MBW and CO₂ at zero response (the reference index) reduced methane by 0.61 g day⁻¹ (95% interval 0.46–0.75), 46% (35–54%) of the largest attainable methane reduction (−1.34 g day⁻¹; −1.46 to −1.22). Holding MBW alone at zero gave a similar reduction (0.63 g day⁻¹) but increased CO₂ by 10.0 g day⁻¹. Within the region, the weighting sets the balance between the three traits: an index that increased MBW by 0.12 kg⁰·⁷⁵ and CO₂ by 20.7 g day⁻¹ still reduced CH₄ by 0.52 g day⁻¹, and some methane reduction remained possible up to an MBW increase of about 0.65 kg⁰·⁷⁵ (0.52–0.77), or a CO₂ increase of about 75 g day⁻¹ (63–86) (Figure 1).

## Discussion

The present study estimated genetic parameters for a comprehensive set of methane trait definitions in pasture-based sheep, comparing absolute, ratio-based, and residual methane traits. Multiple methane traits are used across the literature to quantify emissions, but their statistical properties, and their genetic relationships with production traits, are not always made explicit. Lassen and Difford \[24\] highlighted the need for further empirical work on the genetic parameters of ratio- and residual-based methane traits. This study addresses that gap and extends it by examining how these traits respond to selection within a selection index.

### Genetic parameters of methane trait definitions

Heritability estimates across the nine methane trait definitions ranged from 0.12 to 0.46, indicating that methane production carries meaningful additive genetic variation whichever way it is expressed. These estimates lie within the range reported for methane traits in sheep \[10, 38\], beef \[7, 19\] and dairy cattle \[9, 39\], and are comparable in magnitude to traits already used in livestock breeding programmes, such as somatic cell count \[40\], which has delivered sustained genetic gain in dairy cattle once incorporated into a breeding objective \[41\]. Feed intake is the main determinant of an animal's daily methane output \[42, 43\]. Where intake is recorded, methane is therefore commonly expressed per unit of intake (methane yield) or residualised on intake \[22, 44\]. In grazing sheep, however, feed intake is rarely measured at scale, and scaling or residualising methane against body size, growth or production traits serves as a practical proxy for that missing feed-intake information \[15, 18, 20\]. The adjustment traits used here (MBW, ADG, muscle mass, rumen volume and CO₂) are also biologically relevant in their own right. Maintenance energy requirements, and hence much of an animal's feed intake, are proportional to metabolic body weight, so MBW captures the size-driven component of intake. Average daily gain describes the rate of growth and muscle mass the product of growth, so methane expressed relative to ADG reflects emissions relative to growth rate, and methane relative to muscle mass approximates emissions per unit of saleable product. Rumen volume reflects fermentative capacity and retention time \[45\]. Carbon dioxide output reflects total metabolic activity and is closely related to feed intake in PAC-measured sheep \[15\], therefore CH₄ ratio and RMTMBW+CO₂ approximate methane relative to intake. However, as shown below, scaling or residualising methane on these proxies did not remove its genetic association with body size and growth.

In sheep, the heritability of daily methane was similar to those previously estimated using PAC in New Zealand lambs and ewes (0.19–0.20) \[10\], but lower than respiration-chamber estimates \[38\]. In cattle, daily methane heritability measures range from 0.15 to 0.42 in beef \[7, 19\] and from 0.16 to 0.33 in dairy \[9, 39\], with the highest beef estimates from GreenFeed measurements averaged over a full test period \[7, 19\]. This is consistent with a single short PAC measurement capturing less of an animal's average emission than repeated measurements \[13, 25\]. The heritability of CH₄ ratio fell within the range of 0.17–0.25 reported by Jonker et al. \[10\]. Methane relative to product (0.21–0.66) and residual methane (0.38–0.46) were generally more heritable in cattle \[9, 19\] than the corresponding traits presented here, which adjust for body size, growth and CO₂ as proxies for intake rather than for measured intake or output.

### Genetic relationships with body size, growth and CO₂

Genetically, the alternative methane definitions were strongly correlated with absolute methane in most cases (rg = 0.71–1.00), indicating that they share much of the same underlying additive genetic variation rather than capturing a materially distinct trait. Methane per kg of average daily gain (CH₄/ADG) was the most genetically distinct definition (rg = 0.38 with absolute methane), consistent with the ratio being dominated by its small and highly variable denominator, ADG (rg = −0.81), which is also estimated over a window of up to ±120 days rather than at the time of the methane measurement. Daily methane was also strongly and positively genetically correlated with body size and growth: metabolic body weight (rg = 0.72), live weight (rg = 0.71), and average daily gain (rg = 0.51). Ratio and residual methane traits did not remove these genetic relationships. Methane intensity and the residual methane traits were negatively genetically correlated with their own denominator or adjustment trait, while remaining strongly genetically correlated with absolute methane itself (rg = 0.78–1.00 for the three residual traits). This is consistent with the general statistical behaviour of ratio and residual variables described by Kennedy et al. \[46\] and Kronmal \[47\]. This is expected, as a phenotypic residual is not genetically independent of the traits it is regressed on unless the regression is applied at the genetic level.

Carbon dioxide production was moderately heritable (h² = 0.25) and strongly genetically correlated with both daily methane (rg = 0.57 ± 0.04) and metabolic body weight (rg = 0.83 ± 0.02). CO₂ output reflects total heat production and hence energy intake, and CO₂ is strongly correlated with feed intake in PAC-measured sheep \[15, 48\] and in cattle \[49, 50\]. However, more efficient animals produce less CO₂ per unit of intake \[51\], which limits its accuracy as a proxy for intake.

### Heterogeneous residual variance

Genetic evaluations can assume a common residual variance across contemporary groups. This assumption is rarely met, and when it is violated, records from more variable groups receive more weighting and variance components and breeding values are biased \[30, 52, 53\]. It is particularly relevant to PAC methane: between-day and between-group variation in PAC measurements is large \[25\], emissions differ with site, pasture and protocol \[12, 13\], and the present data combined animals of very different ages and physiological stages. Accounting for this heterogeneity lowered the heritability of methane, CH₄/MBW, RMTMBW and RMTMBW+CO₂, and of CO₂, relative to a single pooled residual variance (results not shown). However classifying the residual variance as either homogeneous or heterogeneous had little impact on the genetic correlations, for example the genetic correlation between methane and methane intensity was 0.85 in the full data, and also 0.85 in both young and mature animals when analysed separately, and the young-versus-mature genetic correlation for methane was 0.99. Residual heterogeneity therefore mainly affects heritability estimates and should be accounted for whenever genetic parameters are estimated from PAC methane recorded across physiological stages. However, only 25.4% of animals had more than one methane record, which limits the precision of the permanent environmental variance and prevented its estimation for the computed-tomography traits and RMTADG.

### Methane traits in a selection index

Alternative methane traits have been frequently proposed as selection criteria \[22, 44, 54, 55\], but the central result of the index analysis is that the choice of methane trait is of secondary importance. A favourable region, in which methane is reduced without a decline in body size or CO₂, does exist, and every methane trait examined, whether absolute, ratio or residual, reached the same response within it once appropriate weights on MBW and CO₂ were included in the breeding goal; all then led to the same index (Table 6). A ratio or residual trait is, in effect, a fixed weighting of methane against its denominator or adjustment trait \[34, 46\]. It is therefore the weighting of methane, body size and CO₂ in the breeding goal, not the methane trait definition, that determines whether methane can be reduced without reducing size.

This agrees with the observation that direct multi-trait selection can match or outperform selection on a ratio \[56–58\], and with the long-standing debate over ratio, residual and index selection for feed efficiency \[59, 60\]. Within the region, the balance can be shifted towards slightly higher body size or intake, as proxied by CO₂, at the cost of a smaller methane reduction; the reference index, which holds both at zero response, is the neutral choice. The weights reported here depend on the genetic parameters and are specific to this population. They were also treated as constant across generations, although genetic parameters change under selection (Cuyabano et al. \[61\]). Responses are per generation at a fixed selection intensity, whereas realised selection intensity depends on the breeding programme, the recording of methane on only part of the population, and the use of genomic information, none of which were modelled.

The weights on MBW and CO₂ in the present index are not economic values but those required to reduce methane without changing MBW or CO₂, similar to a desired-gains approach \[37\]. An economic breeding goal would instead weight each trait, including methane, by its economic value \[62–64\], but the same principles apply. Feed intake is rarely recorded at scale in grazing sheep, so data sufficient to include it directly in a selection index are unlikely to become available, and proxies such as CO₂ are likely to remain necessary. Economic values on an alternative methane trait are therefore subject to the same constraints identified in this analysis: the trait already carries implicit weights on its component traits (here MBW and CO₂), which must be accounted for when those traits are weighted, and the correlated responses of the index should be checked against the attainable responses (Figure 1) to avoid an unintended cost to production.

## Conclusions

This study estimated genetic parameters for nine alternative methane trait definitions in a large, pasture-based sheep population, and shows that these definitions differ meaningfully in their genetic parameters and in their genetic relationships with body size and growth. All definitions were heritable, with estimates ranging from low to moderate (h² = 0.12–0.46). Ratio and residual methane traits remained strongly genetically correlated with absolute daily methane, showing that they share much of the same underlying genetic variation rather than representing a genetically distinct trait. Daily methane itself was strongly and positively genetically correlated with body size and growth, and scaling or residualising methane redistributed this genetic association rather than removing it. When a methane trait is used on its own, the choice of definition therefore determines the direction and magnitude of the genetic relationship between methane and production.

The selection index analysis shows, however, that this choice matters far less than how methane is weighted against body size and CO₂. In an index of methane, metabolic body weight and CO₂, once appropriate weights on body size and CO₂ were included, every trait, whether absolute, ratio or residual, reached the same favourable response and the same index. The weighting of methane, body size and CO₂ in the breeding goal, not the methane trait definition, therefore determines whether methane can be reduced without reducing size. In practice these weights would come from economic values, and the same framework identifies the minimum value that must be placed on methane, relative to body size, for an economic index to achieve this.
