# Response to Reviewers — GSEV-D-26-00126

Oct 1, 2026 · @Dermot Kelly

## Overview

Manuscript GSEV-D-26-00126, “Genetic Parameters and Selection Responses for Alternative Methane Trait Definitions in Pasture-Based Sheep”, Genetics Selection Evolution. Revision due 27 Oct 2026.

We thank the Editor and both reviewers for their careful and constructive reading of our manuscript. Below we reproduce each comment in full followed by our response. In brief, we have (i) re-examined the genetic-parameter analysis in detail and added carbon dioxide as a trait in its own right; (ii) re-estimated the parameters used in the selection index analysis from a single trivariate model of methane (CH4), metabolic body weight (MBW) and CO2, and rebuilt the index analysis on them; and (iii) refocused that analysis on how alternative methane traits themselves operate within an index. All changes made in response to the reviewers' comments are highlighted in the revised manuscript. Line numbers given in our responses refer to the revised, highlighted manuscript, whereas line numbers in the reviewers' comments refer to the original submission.&#32;

> Dealing with each in turn. A substantial part of the paper deals with description of the data set and estimation of genetic parameters. **Response:** We thank the reviewer for raising this. On checking Jonker et al. (2018), we found that their ratio trait is defined as CH4/(CH4 + CO2) on a molar basis (mol/mol), whereas our submission calculated it on a mass basis (g/g), which we have made more clear now. We have recalculated the trait on a molar basis, which raises the heritability from 0.10 to 0.13 under a homogeneous residual variance. We separately found that allowing residual variance to differ across contemporary-group-mean classes raises the mass-basis heritability further, to 0.19, closely matching Jonker et al.'s lamb estimate corroborated by an independent, component-based derivation of the same trait's heritability from the underlying methane and CO2 (co)variances, which gives a very similar value (\~0.19). In light of this, and of our reflection on the overall modelling strategy prompted by the other reviewers' comments (in particular on combining growing and mature animals, addressed below), the revised manuscript now uses the heterogeneous-residual model as the primary analysis for CH4 ratio: its estimate (0.19) is the value reported in Table 2 and the basis of the discussion, with the homogeneous-residual results given in this response for reference only. We believe the units difference and the residual-variance structure, explain the great majority of the original discrepancy, and we now discuss this explicitly in the manuscript. We also note that Jonker et al.'s data come from a different population (New Zealand lambs and ewes) than our own (Irish pasture-based sheep of multiple breeds), which alone would be expected to produce some residual difference in heritability. The corresponding repeatability under this model is 0.32 (Table 2). The homogeneous-residual estimates above (0.10 ± 0.01 on a mass basis; 0.13 on a molar basis) are for the reviewer's information only and are not reported in the manuscript. The full re-analysis is described in the Methods, with further supporting detail in Supplementary Material S1.

> It appears that PAC data from different aged animals was combined in the analysis and these contemporary groups would have differed widely in liveweights and gas production, however, there was no discussion around scaling to normalise the variances of the contemporary groups nor even any discussion if this was examined.

**Response:** This is correct, and we thank the reviewer for raising it. We have now tested directly whether a single combined analysis across growing and mature animals is appropriate. Residual variance heterogeneity across contemporary-group-mean classes was fitted for the methane traits with sufficient data spanning multiple stages; this is described in the Methods and Discussion. We also fitted a model in which residual variance differed by physiological stage but, on reflection, chose contemporary-group-mean classes instead, because residual variance in methane traits tends to scale with the mean, and contemporary-group means vary widely within as well as between stages (e.g. with season, diet and measurement date). Grouping residuals by contemporary-group mean therefore captures this scale effect more fully, while still largely absorbing the stage difference, as contemporary groups are largely stage-specific. Where the heterogeneous-residual model converged (five of the nine methane traits), its heritability and repeatability estimates are now the ones reported in Table 2. For the reviewer’s information, the corresponding heritabilities (SE) under a single homogeneous residual variance were 0.25 (0.02) for CH4, 0.19 (0.02) for CH4/MBW, 0.10 (0.01) for CH4 ratio, 0.18 (0.02) for RMTMBW and 0.18 (0.02) for RMTMBW+CO2; the genetic correlations remain from homogeneous-residual bivariate models, as a heterogeneous-residual bivariate model was not estimable. We also estimated the genetic correlation between methane production measured on growing animals and on mature animals, treated as two separate but genetically linked traits; this bivariate genetic correlation was 0.99, indicating that, genetically, methane production in young and old animals is essentially the same trait, which supports the appropriateness of the combined analysis.

> In summary, if this was just a genetic parameter estimation paper additional information would have been available to check if there was any underlying issues with the analysis and the values would have been formally compared and contrasted with other publications. This was not done, but the parameters estimated in at least one case, diverge markedly from previous published work and this has not been commented on. I suggest the analysis is re-examined and some additional results from the parameter estimation process are included at least in the supplemental material.

**Response:** This is now addressed throughout: the discrepancy with Jonker et al. is discussed explicitly (see above and the Discussion section), and substantially more detail on how the genetic-parameter analysis was conducted and independently verified is now given in the Methods and Supplementary Material S1. Table 3 now reports the genetic and phenotypic correlations of CO2 and of each alternative definition with daily methane from the re-estimated models; the correlations among the alternative definitions themselves were not re-estimated and have been removed.

> The second issue relates to the selection of alternative methane trait definitions, including residual trait definitions. These on the surface appear arbitrary, but in fact, all they are doing are trying to correct for differences in feed intake using “proxy traits” as feed intake has not been measured. It is the amount of methane produced per unit feed intake and the amount of production per unit of feed intake that most closely relates to the question at hand “how can I maximise production of lamb carcass weight per hectare while reducing methane emissions” I feel this aspect has been not directly confronted, because there is an important additional caveat: around 70% of the feed is used in ewe maintenance and reproduction and only 30% in lamb carcass production. So increased fecundity, ewe longevity and lamb survival and associated traits relationship with methane traits are probably more important than liveweight or average daily gain in an enterprise's bio-economic analysis.

**Response:** We accept the reviewer’s point. The ratio and residual trait definitions are, in effect, proxies for feed-intake information that this dataset does not contain. In response, the revised manuscript now uses CO2, rather than average daily gain (ADG), as the proxy for feed intake, as CO2 output reflects total metabolic activity and is more directly linked to intake than growth rate. Beyond the index analysis, the revised manuscript also addresses these points through the genetic-parameter results. The biological rationale for each ratio and residual definition, including the feed-intake information each stands in for, is now set out in the Background and Discussion. CO2 is reported as a trait in its own right, with its heritability and its genetic and phenotypic correlations with methane and MBW, so readers can see how closely the intake proxy tracks methane and body size. The Discussion also notes that the residual traits are strongly genetically correlated with absolute methane (0.78–1.00), so they are not genetically independent of the traits they are adjusted for. Nevertheless, a genuine bio-economic analysis would need to weigh feed intake per unit of production directly, accounting for the fact that roughly 70% of a ewe flock’s feed intake supports ewe maintenance and reproduction rather than lamb carcass production; fecundity, ewe longevity and lamb survival are plausibly at least as important as the liveweight- and growth-based traits examined here. This limitation is stated in the Discussion. The selection index analysis in the revised manuscript is not an attempt to build the breeding objective the reviewer describes: it includes no feed intake, no fecundity, longevity or survival traits and no economic weights, and its results are not presented as the response to selection under a national breeding index. Its purpose is narrower: given how widely alternative methane traits are now proposed, it examines how absolute, ratio and residual methane traits operate within an index, and shows that these definitions are largely redundant, as each can achieve the same response once the accompanying traits are weighted appropriately. We now state this aim in the Abstract, Background, Methods and Discussion. Within that aim, the analysis shows directly what using body size as a proxy does: a ratio or residual trait is, in effect, a fixed weighting of methane against body size. A favourable region, in which methane is reduced without a decline in MBW or CO2, does exist (Figure 1), and it can be reached from any of the methane traits once appropriate weights on MBW and CO2 are included in the breeding goal (Table 6). Whichever methane trait definition is used, the same favourable response is reached once MBW and CO2 are weighted appropriately, so the choice between definitions matters less than how the accompanying traits are weighted. In line with the reviewer’s point on feed cost, CO2 is treated as a proxy for intake and used only as a safeguard: the reference favourable index holds both MBW and CO2 at zero response, so that methane is not reduced by favouring animals that eat less, nor is higher intake favoured. A bio-economic analysis including feed intake, fecundity, longevity and survival would need parameters for those traits and is beyond the scope of this paper.

> The final issue relates to the use of the smith-hazel index theory used and this has two facets. The first is in several places the authors talk about progress per generation, but as far as I can understand from the paper (line 243, this actually equates to a selection intensity of i=1). I have no problem with this but would suggest the authors be more explicit throughout. In practice in sheep, the selection intensity per generation is likely to be closer to 1.7 and the generation length around 2.8 years. The second aspect that is not acknowledged in the paper is that in a real flock, not all animals may be measured for all traits, measurements may differ between sexes, and progeny, and genomics may or may not or only partially be used and if it is used the accuracy of the breeding values differ as well. The impacts of this are not explored here, and that is okay, but there does need to be acknowledgement along with references that these are real issues in practice and you cannot simply extrapolate from the graphs presented to a real world situation. The final aspect is that the use of Smith-Hazel index theory is at best an approximation and this is explored in Cuyabano, Beatriz C. D., Mariana R. Motta, Jeremie Vandenplas, Nancy L. Garcia, Fatima Shokor, Pascal Croiseau, Didier Boichard, Sophie Aguerre, and Sophie Mattalia. 2025. “Trajectories of Genetic Correlations in Populations under Selection: From Theory to a Case-Study.” bioRxiv. https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.03.13.643026. There needs to be some caveats and a reference to this work, as this issue is becoming more important with the use of genomics.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for these points, which we have addressed in the revised selection index analysis (Methods, “Selection index analysis”; Results, Figure 1 and Table 6; Supplementary Tables S2–S4).

1. Selection intensity: responses are now stated explicitly, in the Methods and in every table and figure caption, as per generation at a selection intensity of i = 1. Supplementary Table S2 gives the same responses at i = 1.7 and per year with a generation interval of 2.8 years (scaling by 1.7 and 0.61, respectively), as illustrative values.
2. Partial recording, sex, progeny and genomics: the analysis assumes selection on the candidate’s own records for all three traits, with no relatives’ or genomic information, no sex differences and no differences in breeding-value accuracy; we now state that these are limitations.
3. Approximation: we cite Cuyabano et al. (2025) \[42\] and state that the genetic parameters were treated as constant across generations, whereas they change under selection. We now propagate the sampling uncertainty of the parameter estimates into the index results by Monte Carlo simulation: the 15 (co)variance parameters of the trivariate model were resampled jointly (10,000 draws) from a multivariate normal distribution using their REML estimates and sampling (co)variance matrix, and results are reported with 95% intervals (Methods). We also state that the weights reported are specific to the parameters estimated in this population and should not be generalised.
4. Scope: we make clear throughout that the analysis is not intended as a large, sophisticated index simulation or a national breeding index. It is a deliberately narrow, detailed investigation of how methane traits behave within an index, and the simplifying assumptions above follow from that aim. Within this scope, we believe the conclusions on the relative behaviour of absolute, ratio and residual methane traits hold, even though the absolute responses should not be read as predictions for a real breeding programme.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this point and agree the original submission did not explain the biological rationale for the residual and ratio trait definitions, including regressing methane on CO2 production. We have kept the Methods section focused on describing the approach taken (how each trait was constructed), and have placed the biological rationale for these trait definitions in the Background and Discussion sections instead, where we believe it is more naturally read alongside the interpretation of the results. We have also now included CO2 as a trait in its own right throughout the analysis: descriptive statistics, heritability and genetic standard deviation, and its genetic and phenotypic correlations with methane and metabolic body weight are all now reported, along with a biological interpretation of what these relationships suggest about the animals' overall energy metabolism. CO2 is also included in the selection index analysis, both as a recorded trait and in the breeding goal; CH4 ratio, which when linearised gives the same breeding-goal direction as CH4/CO2, is among the methane traits examined in the index (Table 6). We do not present CO2 as a validated proxy for feed intake.

> Regarding the PAC method - what are pros and cons regarding you study? Does that capture the real emission of the animals? And how do you address the in general very little amount of repeatable records per animal?

**Response:** The pros and cons of the PAC method for this study are now discussed in the Background. In brief, PACs are portable, quick (about one hour per measurement) and inexpensive, so large numbers of animals can be measured on farm under commercial pasture-based conditions, which is what makes a genetic evaluation of this size feasible; PAC measurements are also repeatable and related to respiration-chamber measurements. Their main limitation is that each record is a short snapshot taken off pasture, rather than a full 24-hour measurement, so it is sensitive to time since feeding and diurnal variation, and it does not capture feed intake directly. PAC records therefore rank animals for methane output well, but do not give an exact daily emission for each animal. On the reviewer's point about the generally small number of repeated records per animal: we acknowledge this as a real limitation of the dataset and now state this explicitly. At the same time, we believe it remains important to account for a permanent environmental effect in the model even with a modest proportion of repeated records, given the amount of occasion-to-occasion variability observed in methane measurements; omitting it would risk attributing repeatable but non-genetic variation to the additive genetic variance. We report repeatability alongside heritability for every trait for which the permanent environmental variance could be estimated, and note directly in the results where it could not (itself a further indication of how sparse repeated records limit what can be estimated for some of the smaller trait subsets, such as the CT-derived traits).

> I would also like you to mention in the discussion-section how you findings relate to other study´s findings. Are they somewhat similar? Or are there any surprises? And as you mention - Ireland is a grass-based system. Are your results transferable to other production systems?

**Response:** We agree. The Discussion now compares our heritability estimates with published estimates for daily methane, CH4 ratio, methane relative to product and residual methane in sheep, beef and dairy cattle. The heritability of daily methane matched PAC estimates in New Zealand lambs and ewes (0.19–0.20; Jonker et al., 2018) and was lower than the sheep respiration-chamber estimate (0.29). In cattle, the highest estimates come from full-test GreenFeed averages, consistent with a single short PAC measurement capturing less of an animal's average emission. The CH4 ratio heritability now falls within the 0.17–0.25 range reported by Jonker et al. (2018). Methane relative to product (0.21–0.66) and residual methane (0.38–0.46) were generally more heritable in cattle than the corresponding traits here, which adjust for body size, growth and CO2 as proxies for intake rather than for measured intake or output. On transferability, the Discussion notes that our data come from Irish pasture-based flocks of several breeds, recorded at grazing in lambs, hoggets and ewes. Heritabilities for daily methane and CH4 ratio were similar to those of PAC-measured grazing sheep in New Zealand and Australia, and sire rankings for PAC methane have been maintained between grazing and controlled environments (Wahinya et al., 2022). This suggests the parameters apply to other grazing sheep systems, while transfer to housed or concentrate-fed systems, where diet and intake differ, remains to be tested.

> At last - there are other studies showing differences between growing and adult animals in their physiology and therefore also in their methane emissions. Did you consider splitting the dataset into different groups? That might affect your results.

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this point. As described in our response to Reviewer 1 above, we now formally test this through the heterogeneous-residual-variance analysis and the genetic correlation between methane production in growing and mature animals (0.99). Together, these support a combined analysis for this dataset and directly answer the reviewer's question about whether growing and adult animals differ physiologically in their methane emissions.
